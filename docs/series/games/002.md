# Video 002: Every mistake moves the line

The question is 001's landing question, unchanged: Mario is in the air;
will he land within the next 10 frames? The neuron reads his depth
(`$00CE`) and vertical speed (`$009F`); the answer key is his float state
(`$001D`). Addresses, ROM and verification are in `001.md`. What's new is
who chooses the border: in 001 we searched every leaning line and kept the
best; here the neuron moves its own line, one moment at a time.

## The rules

Nate's `ndouglas/linear-regression` (built from Serrano, *Grokking Machine
Learning*) has three tricks for fitting a line *through* points. 002 needs
a line *between* two colours of dot, so each trick is translated. Labels
are −1 (doesn't land soon) and +1 (lands soon); the line says +1 when
w·x + b > 0.

| In the repo (fitting) | For a border | The nudge, after looking at one moment |
|---|---|---|
| Simple trick | Simple trick | Only if wrong: move each weight a fixed step by the *sign* of its input |
| Absolute trick | Perceptron rule (Rosenblatt, 1958) | Only if wrong: move the weights by the input, times a fixed step |
| Square trick | Widrow–Hoff / LMS (1960) | Always: move the weights by the input, times the error (label − w·x − b) |

To check before any of it is said: the dates and attributions, and that
Serrano's perceptron and logistic tricks match these forms.

## Recon (2026-11-01, scratch script; not yet checked)

A throwaway run, to decide whether the episode is worth shaping around
it. Every number here is to be redone in `tools/series/lab/exp002.py`
with checks before it's quoted. Trained on sessions 1–2 (16,135 moments
in the air), tested on session 3 (8,072; lands-soon rate 25.4%). Inputs
standardized on the training moments; weights start at zero; 30 passes,
each visiting the moments in a shuffled order; 10 orders per rule.

| Rule | Test score | Settles? |
|---|---|---|
| Search (001) | 90.8% | (no learning) |
| Simple trick, per moment, step 0.01 | 74.6–89.4% across orders | No: last 10 passes swing ~25 points, ~117° |
| Perceptron, per moment, step 0.01 | 70.2–89.6% | No: ~17 points, ~127° |
| Square trick, per moment, step 0.001 | 86.7–88.1% | Nearly: ~1 point, ~3° |
| Square trick, whole batch, step 0.1 | 87.1% | Yes, by step ~10; equals the least-squares solution |
| Logistic trick, whole batch | 88.1% | Yes |

- One perceptron run's test score after each of passes 21–30: 79.0, 86.3,
  74.6, 73.1, 85.7, 82.8, 85.3, 88.5, 87.0, 89.6%.
- Dropping 30 random moments (of 16,135) and rerunning the perceptron
  with the same order: 68.9–86.0% over 10 drops.
- Direction, in standardized units: least squares ~65°, logistic ~64°,
  search ~30°. The settling rules lean differently from search's line,
  not just score lower. (To recheck: the angle comparison, and whether
  coding labels 0/1 instead of −1/+1 changes the batch square result.)
- From a zero start the perceptron's step size doesn't change the border
  at all, only the weights' size: 001's "scaling the weights doesn't move
  the border".

## The episode's spine (Nate, 2026-11-01)

- Be honest about the wobble: the perceptron aims at the right thing
  (mistakes) but can't settle, because with "mostly on one side" some
  moment is always wrong.
- The square trick settles, but aims at something slightly different
  (squared error, which also listens to moments it already gets right),
  and stops short of search's line.
- Why the settling kind won: its nudges can be averaged, so every
  moment's nudge can be computed at once (a batch; what GPUs are for), and
  the same property is what lets learning reach through many layers later.
  (Nate had assumed the repo's last trick was the GPU-friendly one; it's
  the trick's *family*, run on batches, not the per-point version.)
