# Video 002: Every mistake moves the line

**The question** (from draft 13, after Nate's interview): Mario is short
of the line where the screen starts to follow him; will the screen move
within the next half second (30 frames)? 001's landing question was
dropped for 002: its numbers point the wrong way for a lay viewer
(depth grows downward, rising speed is negative, and standardizing made
every below-average number negative), so every nudge was a sign puzzle.
Here both numbers are never negative and bigger means sooner, and they
aren't standardized.

| | Address | Meaning | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Input 1 | `$0755` | Mario's position on screen as the scroll code reads it (`Player_Pos_ForScroll`), in pixels; used ÷ 16, in blocks | Data Crystal; SMBDIS |
| Input 2 | `$0057` | Horizontal speed, signed, in sixteenths of a pixel a frame (`Player_X_Speed`): at most 24 walking, 40 running (`MaxRightXSpdData`); used ÷ 16, in pixels a frame | Data Crystal; SMBDIS |
| Answer key | `$0775` | Pixels the screen scrolled this frame (`ScrollAmount`); the moment says yes if any of the next 30 frames is non-zero | SMBDIS |
| Play | `$000E` | Player state, 08 in play | 001.md |

`ScrollHandler` moves the screen only once `Player_Pos_ForScroll` is at
least 80 (`cmp #$50`), and a little slower than Mario until 112 (`cmp
#$70`). All verified on `roms2/super_mario_bros.nes` by tests in
`src/nes/gym/series/ram.rs`: the screen never moves before 80, walking
tops out at 24, running at 40, walking left is negative.

**The moments**: every frame in play where Mario is left of 80 and not
walking left (speed ≥ 0), with the game in play through the next 30
frames and no restart inside them. Past 80 the answer is just "is he
walking right"; walking left, the screen can't move until he turns
round (it did within 30 frames for 6.5% of those moments, council round
4), and his speed would be negative, breaking "up means up".

**Recordings**: the random player of 001 (it holds Right about 74% of
frames and B, to run, about 20%; only 2% of moments are faster than
walking's 1.5 pixels a frame) (`examples/series_record.rs`,
`smb1 human 18000`), seeds 1–6; seeds 4–6 were recorded for 002
(2026-11-01). Learned on 1, 2, 4, 5; scored on 3 and 6.

## The rules

Nate's `ndouglas/linear-regression` (built from Serrano, *Grokking Machine
Learning*) has three tricks for fitting a line *through* points. 002 needs
a line *between* two colours of dot, so each trick is translated. Labels
are −1 (the screen doesn't move soon) and +1 (it does); the line says +1 when
w·x + b > 0.

| In the repo (fitting) | For a border | The nudge, after looking at one moment |
|---|---|---|
| Simple trick | Simple trick | Only if wrong: move each weight a fixed step by the *sign* of its input |
| Absolute trick | Perceptron rule (Rosenblatt, 1958) | Only if wrong: move the weights by the input, times a fixed step |
| Square trick | Widrow–Hoff / LMS (1960) | Always: move the weights by the input, times the error (label − w·x − b) |

The simple trick here is Nate's repo's (a fixed step by the input's
sign). Serrano's own simple trick moves by small *random* amounts, with
no learning rate; his perceptron and logistic tricks match the forms
above (checked in his code, `luisguiserrano/manning`, chapters 3, 5, 6).

## The experiment (`tools/series/lab/exp002.py`)

Numbers in `tools/series/videos/002-every-mistake/numbers.json` (this
section is copied from it; the file wins if they differ); the rules are
in `tools/series/lab/rules.py`, tested in
`tools/series/tests/test_rules.py`. 10,720 moments learned from, 4,883
scored (the screen moved soon on 51%). Inputs in blocks and pixels a
frame, not standardized; weights start at zero; 30 passes, each
visiting every moment once in a shuffled order; 10 orders per rule. The
perceptron's step is 2⁻⁷ so that 8× is exact; the square trick's 0.001
(one moment at a time) and 0.1 (whole batch).

Why not the raw bytes: in pixels and sixteenths, the bias learns about
30,000 times more slowly than the weights (the least and greatest
eigenvalues of the inputs' second moments, with the bias's 1), and the
square trick was still drifting and wobbling 3–8 points after 30 passes.
In blocks and pixels a frame (both bytes ÷ 16) it settles: within 0.3%
of least squares, wobbling about a point or two.

The data:

- The training moments sit on 1,313 spots; 232 hold both answers, and
  27% of moments sit on one; the best possible on the training moments
  is 95%. 53% of moments are on spots holding 50 or more; the biggest,
  710 moments, all "no", is Mario at 2.5 blocks (40 pixels, where each
  life starts) not moving sideways; 28% of them are in the air.
- What the neuron can't see: the player's next presses, among other
  things. Where search's border wrongly says yes, he held Right for 31%
  of the next half second; where it rightly says yes, 81% (where it's
  right at all, 78%; where it misses a yes, 99%). Not the whole reason
  (council round 4): at spots holding both answers, "no" moments still
  had Right held 59% of the window, many falling just short (median
  furthest 79 px); adding the share of Right held as a third input lifts
  a straight border's fit only from 90% to 94%. At the training points
  holding both answers, Right was held 88% of the window when the
  screen moved and 59% when it didn't (the script's figures; at the
  test points, 88% and 53%). The game
  also keeps a finer part of the position the two bytes don't show
  (`$0400`, SprObject_X_MoveForce, sixteenths of a pixel; it takes all
  16 values): at 66% of the points where Mario is moving it differs
  between moments (checked in the run), so a "point" is "the same two
  numbers", not the same state. But the fraction doesn't explain the
  outcome (adversarial T: at mixed moving points the "yes" moments have
  the higher fraction only 115 times of 224, chance; 108 of 244
  same-point-same-fraction cells still hold both answers), so the
  script doesn't offer it as a reason.

- Too big a step: the batch square trick with 2 times its step grows its
  total squared miss at every one of 10 steps, to 137 times where it
  started (checked in the run; draft 13 said 60, the value after 9
  steps, from an off-by-one fixed in round 4). For the first steps no
  total crosses its target; it's the weights that overshoot the bowl's
  bottom (the inputs' steep direction: a stable step is below about
  0.16, and 0.2 is just past it). The step that settled, 0.1, also
  crosses the bottom along the steepest direction every step, landing
  lower each time; what separates them is landing higher or lower.
- The perceptron: one run's last ten passes 90%, 88%, 85%, 90%, 82%,
  87%, 84%, 90%, 86%, 67%. That run (the first order) is the outlier:
  the other nine orders ended at 82%–90% (checked in the run: the run
  shown ends on its lowest pass, lowest of all orders). Shrinking the
  step (council rounds 5–6, not in the script): rate / k at the k-th
  moment swings a median 0.16 points over the last ten passes, but only
  because the step is 1/10,000 of its start by pass 2, so the border
  stays about where the first pass left it (86–89% after pass 1, 87–90%
  at the end). rate / p, rate / √k and rate / √p still swing 12–14;
  rate / p² swings 0.8 and halving each pass 0. What matters is how far
  the step shrinks, not how often. Robbins–Monro promises only a drift
  toward the perceptron measure's lowest point, zero. The script says
  "with a fixed step" instead (step-size-2's "this rule", tldr). Same order, 30 moments left out: 66%–87%
  (none left out: 67%). Control, on only the moments search's border
  gets right: 100%, last moving in pass 100 of 300 (the nearest moment
  is 0.0006 blocks from search's border, and Novikoff's bound grows as
  that gap shrinks). Council round 4: search's cut sits at a data
  quantile, so that gap is near zero by construction; the widest gap any
  border leaves is about 0.0016–0.0018 blocks across. The bound is
  consistent with 100 passes but doesn't explain the number, so the
  script no longer ties the two.
- Its weights, just before each nudge of the last ten passes, are a
  median 51 steps from zero, a nudge 3.37 steps long: about 15 nudges
  from zero. But the position weight is about 3 of its own nudges from
  zero, so each nudge changes it by about 35% (the speed weight, 3%):
  the border's tilt keeps changing. (The landing data's weights were
  about 2 nudges from zero in all.)
- Who moves it (last ten passes, median of ten orders): the busiest
  tenth of moments make 57% of nudges; 64% of them are moments search's
  border gets wrong, against 8% of all moments; 64% never nudge it.
  Moments search's border gets wrong cause 43% of the late nudges.
- Remembering: the best of each run's pass-end lines (judged on the
  moments learned from) scores 90%–91%; averaging every line 90%.
- The settled borders answer differently from search's on 10% (square),
  7% (logistic) and 7% (hinge) of moments. 18% of moments are right by
  too much for least squares; removing that pull (hinge) gains about one
  point of the three, so it isn't the whole cause.
- The two maps (every border as angle × offset from the map's middle,
  angles from 310° round to 670°, so the bowl is one piece on the left
  and the flat shelf, about 120°–307°, fills the right (checked: the
  sheet's edge columns are under 5% flat);
  2.5 blocks and 1.25 pixels a frame;
  `target/series/002-every-mistake/maps.npz`): the mistakes map's lowest
  point scores 91%, the squared-error map's 88%. 7% of neighbouring
  offset steps change the count by 50 or more (largest 1,645). The
  squared-error map (each border at its best size, never negative) is
  flat on 50% of the grid and has exactly one low point elsewhere
  (checked in the run). 82% of the flat borders get most moments wrong,
  against 18% of the sloped ones; the other way round, 83% of the
  borders that get most moments wrong are flat. That 83% depends on how
  far the sheet's offsets reach (95%, 82%, 68% at ±1.5, ±3, ±6), so the
  script gives no number for it.
- 91% against 88% holds on every way to hold out two of the six
  recordings (search ahead by 1.6–3.1 points; 3 and 6 give the largest
  gap) and in leave-one-recording-out (1.4–3.5).
- Angles between borders are not quoted: they depend on how the axes
  are scaled.

Checked before the numbers are written (the run refuses otherwise):

- A step 8 times bigger gives weights exactly 8 times bigger after every
  pass (so the same mistakes, and the same border).
- The batch square trick comes to rest exactly at the least-squares
  solution, solved directly.
- Coding the answers −1/+1 or 0/1 settles on the same square-trick border.
- The squared-error map's lowest point is the least-squares border, and
  it has one low point where it isn't flat.
- On only the moments search's border gets right, the perceptron stops.
- With too big a step, the batch square trick's miss grows at every step.

## The episode's spine (Nate, 2026-11-01; data changed in draft 13)

- The perceptron's rule only reacts to mistakes, with nudges that never
  shrink. No border of any shape gets the moments all right (spots
  holding both answers: the neuron can't see what the player presses
  next), so some moment is always wrong and it never stops; where it
  ends depends on the order and when it's stopped. The control stops,
  after 100 passes.
- It doesn't aim at fewest mistakes. It follows its own measure, how far
  its wrong moments sit on the wrong side (Bishop 4.1.7), lowest with
  every weight at zero; there it says no to everything, so every moment
  where the screen moved pushes it back out. Every nudge leans partly
  back toward zero (checked in the run over the late nudges: its dot
  product with the weights is never positive), so pulls that all lean
  that way can't cancel anywhere but zero, and at zero they push out;
  the weights themselves never come near zero (about 15 nudges out,
  never closer than about 9); batching narrows its score (83–86%) but it moves on every
  step. Its weights stay small,
  the position weight about 3 nudges from zero, so the border's tilt
  keeps jumping.
- The square trick shrinks the total squared miss; its pulls balance at
  the bottom, so, adding up every nudge before each step, it comes to
  rest (one moment at a time it wobbles about 2 points), in nearly the
  same place whatever the order: 88% against 91%, disagreeing on 10% of
  moments. Logistic and a no-pull-past-target version land nearby (89%).
- Fewest mistakes is a staircase: flat between crossings, so no rule can
  walk down it; with many inputs nobody has a fast way to search it
  (Höffgen et al. 1995), which is why learning aims at a measure with a
  slope.
