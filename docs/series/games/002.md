# Video 002: Every mistake moves the line

The question is 001's landing question, unchanged: Mario is in the air;
will he land within the next 10 frames? The neuron reads his depth
(`$00CE`) and vertical speed (`$009F`); the answer key is his float state
(`$001D`). Addresses, ROM and verification are in `001.md`. What's new is
who chooses the border: in 001 we searched every leaning line and kept the
best; here the neuron moves its own line, one moment at a time.

## The rules

Nate's `ndouglas/linear-regression` (built from Serrano, *Grokking Machine
Learning*) has three tricks for fitting a line *through* points. 002 needs
a line *between* two colours of dot, so each trick is translated. Labels
are −1 (doesn't land soon) and +1 (lands soon); the line says +1 when
w·x + b > 0.

| In the repo (fitting) | For a border | The nudge, after looking at one moment |
|---|---|---|
| Simple trick | Simple trick | Only if wrong: move each weight a fixed step by the *sign* of its input |
| Absolute trick | Perceptron rule (Rosenblatt, 1958) | Only if wrong: move the weights by the input, times a fixed step |
| Square trick | Widrow–Hoff / LMS (1960) | Always: move the weights by the input, times the error (label − w·x − b) |

The simple trick here is Nate's repo's (a fixed step by the input's
sign). Serrano's own simple trick moves by small *random* amounts, with
no learning rate; his perceptron and logistic tricks match the forms
above (checked in his code, `luisguiserrano/manning`, chapters 3, 5, 6).

## The experiment (`tools/series/lab/exp002.py`)

Numbers in `tools/series/videos/002-every-mistake/numbers.json` (this
section is copied from it; the file wins if they differ); the rules are
in `tools/series/lab/rules.py`, tested in
`tools/series/tests/test_rules.py`. Learned on sessions 1–2
(16135 moments in the air), scored on session 3
(8072; lands soon 25%). Inputs
standardized on the moments learned from (each its distance from its
average, in its own standard deviation); weights start at zero; 30
passes, each visiting every moment once in a shuffled order; 10 orders
per rule. The perceptron's step is 2⁻⁷ so that 8× is exact.

The data:

- The moments sit on 845 spots (depth and speed are whole
  numbers); 284 spots hold both answers, and
  65% of moments sit on one, so no border of any
  shape is perfect: the best possible on the training moments is
  93%. 57% of moments are on spots
  holding 50 or more; crossing one spot changes a border's mistakes by up
  to 292.

| Rule | Score on session 3 | Settles? |
|---|---|---|
| Search (001) | 91% | (no learning) |
| Simple trick, per moment | 75%–89% at pass 30 | No |
| Perceptron, per moment | 70%–90% at pass 30; 43%–90% over passes 21–30 | No: one run's last 10 passes 73%–90% |
| Square trick, per moment | 87%–88% | Nearly: wobbles about 1 point |
| Square trick, whole batch | 87% | Yes; at least squares |
| Logistic, whole batch | 88% | Yes |
| No pull past the target (squared hinge), batch | 88% | Yes |
| Perceptron, whole batch | 73%–90% typical (10th percentile to top), low 43%, over its last 100 steps | No |

- The perceptron: one run's last ten passes 79%, 86%, 75%, 73%, 86%, 83%, 85%, 88%, 87%, 90%.
  Same order, 30 moments left out: 81%–90%
  (none left out: 90%). Control, on only the moments
  search's border gets right: 100%, and still from pass 4.
- Its weights, just before each nudge of the last ten passes, are a
  median 3 steps from zero; a nudge is a median
  1.57 steps long, so about 2 nudges from zero (the settled
  square trick's weights are 99.8 steps from zero). The batch
  perceptron's weights stay about 1.9 of its own nudges from zero.
- Who moves it (last ten passes, median of ten orders): the busiest tenth
  of moments make 47% of nudges; 71% of them are moments search's
  border gets wrong, against 8% of all moments; 47% never nudge it.
- Remembering: the best of each run's pass-end lines (judged on the
  moments learned from) scores 89%–90%; averaging every line 89%–89%.
- The settled borders answer differently from search's on
  6% (square), 4% (logistic) and 4% (hinge) of moments.
  16% of moments are right by too much for least squares,
  97% of them rising (against 54% of all); removing that pull (hinge)
  closes only a little of the gap, so it isn't the cause.
- The two maps (every border as angle × offset, in standardized units;
  `target/series/002-every-mistake/maps.npz`): the mistakes map's lowest
  point scores 91%, the squared-error map's 87%. 11% of
  neighbouring offset steps change the count by 50 or more (largest
  2539). The squared-error map (each border at its best size, never
  negative) is flat on 50% of the grid, where a border's yes side
  mostly gets the answers backwards, and has exactly one low point
  elsewhere (checked in the run).
- Angles between borders are not quoted: they depend on how the axes
  are scaled (35° standardized is 8° on the map as drawn).

Checked before the numbers are written (the run refuses otherwise):

- A step 8 times bigger gives weights exactly 8 times bigger after every
  pass (so the same mistakes, and the same border).
- The batch square trick comes to rest exactly at the least-squares
  solution, solved directly.
- Coding the answers −1/+1 or 0/1 settles on the same square-trick border.
- The squared-error map's lowest point is the least-squares border, and

- On only the moments search's border gets right, the perceptron stops.

## The episode's spine (Nate, 2026-11-01; revised after council round 1)

- The perceptron's rule only reacts to mistakes, with full-size nudges.
  With "mostly on one side", some moment is always wrong, so it never
  stops, and where it ends depends on the order and when it's stopped.
  Control: on only the moments search's border gets right (a line splits
  them), it stops, 100% right.
- It doesn't aim at fewest mistakes (the first spine said it did; the
  skeptic and cross-examiner caught it). It follows its own score, how
  far its wrong moments are on the wrong side (Bishop 4.1.7), which on
  data no line splits is lowest at all-zero weights. Its weights stay
  about 3 steps from zero (median, last 10 passes), so each nudge swings
  the border far. The batch version's weights stay under 0.05.
- The square trick measures how far off each moment is, settles in the
  same place every time, and that place is close to, not on, the
  fewest-mistakes border: 87% against 91%, disagreeing on 6% of moments.
  Every smooth score tried lands nearby: logistic 88% (4%), and a version
  with no pull past the target 88% (4%). So "right by too much" is not
  the cause; measuring distances instead of counting mistakes is.
- Fewest mistakes is a staircase: flat between crossings, so no rule can
  walk down it; with many inputs nobody has a fast way to search it
  (Höffgen et al. 1995), which is why learning aims at a score with a
  slope.
- Angles between borders are not quoted: they depend on how the axes
  are scaled (35° standardized is 8° on the map as drawn).
- The squared-error map (each border at its best size) is flat on the
  half where borders face the wrong way round, and slopes to one lowest
  point on the other half, with no other dips (checked on the grid).
